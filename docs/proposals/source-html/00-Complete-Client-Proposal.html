Part I

Why this website, and for whom

1.1

The business case

You described the website's purpose as compliance. That's the right starting point, but it undersells what a good site will actually do for you. There are two distinct jobs here:

Compliance & credibility

A verifiable public presence for regulators, banks, and the compliance teams of overseas buyers. When someone is asked to approve a large transfer to a counterparty, they look. A professional site with visible registrations and permits materially smooths that path.

Marketing & closing deals

Something substantial to point a prospective buyer to, instead of a phone number and a trade-directory listing. It works while you sleep, in a time zone where your buyers are awake.

Worth knowing

Publicly available trade records show your shipments going overwhelmingly to buyers based in China. That single fact shapes several recommendations in this document — particularly around how the site should read to a foreign compliance officer, and the case for a Chinese-language version later (Part XII).

1.2

Who the site is written for

Overseas buyers & their compliance teams

Want: proof you are a real, permitted, documented operation. Product specifications. A clear way to make contact.

Banks & regulators

Want: registered company name, address, permit numbers, transparent ownership. Consistency with what's on your paperwork.

Partners, LGUs, and the local community

Want: legitimacy, an overview of operations, evidence of responsible practice.

You

Want: to update content without paying someone every time a certificate is renewed or a shipment milestone is worth announcing.

2.2

What "you can edit it yourself" actually means

You'll sign in to a private administration area and see your pages listed much like folders. Click a page, click the text, retype it, press Save and Publish. Swapping a photo is choosing a new file. Adding a news post is filling in a title, a date, and the body.

That control extends to your branding. Your logo, the small browser icon, product photos, certificate files and every figure on the site are all things you can replace yourself from the same screen — so when a certificate is renewed, a logo is refined, or a new assay figure comes in, you're not waiting on me to publish it.

What you deliberately won't be able to do is break the design. The layout, fonts, colours and structure are fixed by me at build time; your controls are for content. On a site whose main job is to look credible to a bank, that restriction is a feature, not a limitation — there's no way to accidentally leave the Compliance page looking broken.

Included at handover

A short, plain-language guide covering the handful of things you'll actually do — edit a page, swap a photo, publish a news post, and who to contact if something looks wrong. Written for you, not for a developer.

2.3

Design direction

A separate homepage design draft accompanies this proposal, showing the intended look: restrained, industrial, document-led — the visual language of a company that ships bulk commodities, not a consumer brand.

Please read the draft this way

Everything in it is a placeholder — the logo, the icons, the wording, the figures, the photographs and the certificate details. None of it is real information about your company, and none of it is a suggestion about what your branding should be. It exists purely so you can see the structure and feel before committing to anything.

The mark shown in the top corner is a stand-in drawn to occupy the right space. Your actual logo replaces it — and if you don't have one in a usable digital form yet, that's worth discussing, since it's the one item on the list in Part XIV that can't simply be typed in.

The site will be built mobile-first. Most buyer-side contacts will open a link you send them on a phone, often over a mobile connection, so it must load quickly and read well on a small screen before anything else.

Part IV

The technology, and why

4.1

What the site will be built on

The site will use Umbraco, an established open-source content management system, running on Microsoft's .NET platform. In plain terms: Umbraco is the part that gives you the editing screens; .NET is the engine underneath.

No licence fee, ever

Umbraco is free and open-source under an MIT licence. There's no per-user charge, no revenue threshold, and no expiry. Your only recurring cost is hosting.

Genuinely custom

Nothing about the finished site will look like it came from a template gallery. The design is built specifically for your company.

Maintainable long-term

This is the technology I work in professionally every day, so I can support it directly — and it's a mainstream platform with a large pool of other developers if you ever need someone else.

4.2

Why not a website builder like Wix or Squarespace?

They're faster to launch and I considered them seriously. Three reasons they lose here:

  • They look like what they are. Buyers and compliance officers see hundreds of these. A recognisably templated site subtly signals a smaller, less established operation — the exact opposite of what yours needs to convey.
  • You'd be renting, not owning. Your content and design live inside their system. Moving later means rebuilding from scratch.
  • They cost more to run. Roughly $200–470 per year versus around $75, with less control.
4.3

A word on software versions

The site will be built on the current release of Umbraco (version 18) and Microsoft .NET 10. Being current matters for security, since older versions eventually stop receiving fixes.

Something you should know upfront

Umbraco 18 is a standard-support release rather than a long-term-support one. Its security updates run until June 2027, after which the site should be moved to a newer version. Umbraco issues a new major version roughly every six months, so this is best thought of as a modest recurring maintenance task rather than a one-off.

I'm raising it now because it's a real, ongoing consideration rather than something to discover in a year. A longer-support alternative exists (Umbraco 17, supported to late 2028) if you would prefer fewer updates over having the newest version — happy to switch to that if you'd rather. Either way the cost figures in Part IX are unchanged; what differs is how often the underlying platform needs attention.

Part V

How it's built — architecture

5.1

The shape of the system

You may be shown technical terms by other developers, so here's the plain description of what's being built and why it's appropriate.

The formal description is a coupled content-management architecture — a single, self-contained application that both stores your content and produces the pages visitors see. The pages are assembled on the server and sent to the browser as finished HTML.

ChoiceIn plain termsWhy it's right here
One single applicationRather than splitting the site across several separate systemsOne thing to host, back up, secure and update. Half the running cost and far less that can go wrong
Pages built on the serverThe page arrives complete, rather than being assembled inside the visitor's browserGoogle indexes it properly, and it loads fast on a phone over a mobile connection
Deliberately simple internal structureNo elaborate engineering patternsThose patterns exist to manage complex business rules. A website presenting company information has none, so adding them would add cost and risk with no benefit

That last point is a deliberate decision rather than a shortcut. Over-engineering a simple system is a common and expensive mistake; the discipline is in matching the structure to the actual problem.

5.2

How the site stays fast and protected

A copy of each page is held on a worldwide delivery network (Cloudflare) that sits in front of your site. Visitors are served from whichever location is nearest to them, and your actual server is rarely touched at all.

Fast everywhere

A buyer in Tianjin gets the page from a nearby location rather than from a server in the Philippines.

Resilient

The network absorbs traffic surges and malicious attacks before they ever reach your site.

Inexpensive

Because the server itself does very little work, a modest hosting plan is genuinely sufficient. This is what keeps the annual cost near $75.

When you publish a change, that copy is refreshed immediately — you'll see your edits straight away.

Part VII

Security

7.1

What protects the site

Worth being clear about the risk profile first: this site holds no customer accounts, no payment details, and no sensitive records. The only information it receives is enquiry-form messages. That's a deliberately small target, and the protections are sized accordingly — thorough where it matters, without paying for enterprise measures that address risks you don't carry.

Encrypted connections

All traffic over HTTPS, so visitors see the padlock. Certificates renew automatically.

Protected sign-in

Two-factor authentication on your administrator account, plus limits on repeated login attempts.

Firewall & attack filtering

Malicious traffic is filtered before it reaches the site.

Spam-resistant enquiry form

Automated filtering and rate limiting, so the form doesn't become a nuisance channel.

Backups, held off-site

Daily, stored separately from the site itself, and periodically test-restored — an untested backup isn't really a backup.

Data Privacy Act compliance

Published privacy policy, explicit consent on the enquiry form, and minimal retention of personal information (RA 10173).

Almost all of the above costs nothing beyond the hosting already listed. Paid security upgrades exist, but none of them close a gap that matters at this site's size and risk profile — so they're not included, and I'd rather say so than bundle them in.

Included at launch:

  • Each page given its own title and description, written for the terms buyers actually search

A realistic expectation

Search visibility builds over months, not days, and "nickel ore exporter Philippines" is a competitive term. Realistically, most early traffic will come from people you've directed to the site — which is exactly what matters for the compliance and deal-closing purposes. Search visibility is a compounding bonus on top, helped by posting to the News page occasionally.

11.3

What this plan assumes

The plan above holds under the following assumptions. If any turns out differently, it's worth revisiting — none is a problem, but each would change something:

  • Traffic stays modest. A business-to-business site visited by buyers, banks and partners — not consumer-scale volume. A sudden surge would mean moving to the Managed tier.
  • Content changes occasionally, not daily. Monthly-ish updates. Daily publishing would justify the test-environment setup in the Managed tier.
  • The site stays informational. No online payments, customer accounts, or buyer login portal. Any of those changes the security requirements materially and would need separate planning.
  • Your Google Workspace email continues as-is, billed and managed by you, unaffected by this project.
  • One language at launch (English), with the site built so Chinese can be added later without rework (Part XII).
  • Content is supplied by you — text, photos, certificates. I'll draft and structure it, but the underlying facts, figures and documents have to come from your side. Nothing about your business will be invented to fill a gap.
  • I remain available for maintenance, per whichever arrangement in 11.2 we agree. If that ever changes, the platform is mainstream enough that another developer can take it over.

Part XIII

Questions you may have

Will this affect my current email at all?

No. Email and website are controlled by separate settings on your domain, and only the website setting changes. I verify your email is working as part of launch rather than assuming it. See 3.2 for the diagram.

Do I own the website?

Yes — entirely. The domain is registered in your company's name, the content is yours, and the software is open-source with no licence tying you to anyone. If you ever wanted to move to a different developer, everything transfers.

What if you become unavailable?

A fair question to ask of any solo arrangement. The site is built on mainstream Microsoft technology with a large developer pool, deliberately rather than on anything exotic. Everything is documented and stored in a standard version-control system, so another developer can pick it up. I'd rather you have that assurance in writing than take it on trust.

Can I see it before it goes live?

Yes. You'll review it on a private address in Phase 5, before anything is public, and I'll walk you through it. Nothing goes live without your approval.

What if I want changes after launch?

Content changes — text, photos, news, certificates — you do yourself, at no cost. Design or structural changes come to me, under whichever support arrangement we agree in 11.2.

Is it safe to publish our permit numbers?

That's your judgement to make, and worth a conversation. Generally, publishing registration and permit references is standard practice for exporters and actively helps due-diligence checks — it's what a compliance officer is looking for. But you know your regulatory and competitive situation better than I do. Publish only what you're comfortable with; the site works either way.

Why does the site need to be fast if we only have a few visitors?

Because of who those visitors are. A slow, awkward site read by a bank's compliance officer on a phone does real damage; the same site read by a thousand casual visitors wouldn't matter at all. Low traffic, high stakes per visit.

Could we just use the free Google website builder?

You could, and it would cost nothing. But it produces exactly the templated look you told me you wanted to avoid, and it can't run a proper enquiry form or document library. For a site whose purpose is to signal that this is an established operation, it works against you. See Part III.
